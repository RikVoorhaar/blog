## The problem

I finally made the courageous decision to start reading books in Danish. I want to improve
my Danish, and reading the news already works fine, so folding Danish literature into my
daily routine should grow my vocabulary fast.

There was just one problem: Kobo doesn't ship a Danish dictionary. There is no official
dictionary for Danish on the e-reader at all, and the ones you can find to install are
basically Wiktionary. For Dutch or French, Wiktionary is amazing; for Danish it is just not
a great resource. Whatever word you tap in a novel usually simply isn't there. Reading
without a dictionary is hell, and reaching for my phone breaks the flow. I wanted something
quick and easy.

So I had the idea: build a small project that generates a dictionary from the words that
actually occur in the book. Some napkin math suggested it would be cost-effective —
certainly less than the e-book itself.

## The idea

The idea, more completely: take an EPUB — as long as it has no DRM — and extract every word
in it. Many of those are inflections of the same word, so you can lemmatize them down to a
smaller set (more on that later).

Then, for each word, write a prompt containing the Danish word plus the contexts where it
actually occurs in the book — up to a maximum, ideally the sentence before, the sentence
itself, and the sentence after — and force the model to produce a structured output from
which you can generate a dictionary entry. It's boring, which is the point: EPUB in, one
JSON dictionary entry per lemma out.

From there, a program called `dictgen` (I don't know exactly what it does) turns the entries
into the HTML that the e-reader understands.

The good part is that it genuinely works: every word in the book has an entry. I can't
stress enough how useful that is. Even a very good English dictionary wouldn't have this
property. Danish is full of compounds: you can try to decompose them yourself, but it's not
always an option, or obvious, and no dictionary contains every possible compound. Knowing the actual book
changes the calculus — even a fairly long book might only have 10,000–20,000 unique words,
which you can work through in batches.

And because I'm bilingual in Dutch and English, the dictionary can be personalised: each
entry has a Danish definition plus both a Dutch and an English gloss. That is fantastic for
language learning — and you will not find it in a normal dictionary. That kind of
personalisation is only possible with AI.

## A very short dictionary primer

A dictionary actually has a few moving parts. First, the **lemma**: the dictionary form of a
word. Verbs get conjugated and nouns and adjectives get inflected, but the dictionary lists
the base form — *tage*, not *tager*, *tog* or *taget*.

Then there is the **surface form**: the exact string that appears in the book — the thing you
actually tap. The dictionary essentially needs an entry for every surface form, so that a
lookup is just a map from surface to dictionary entry.

Which is exactly why the lemma matters here. An EPUB only gives you surface forms. You could
generate an entry for each surface directly, but then you would produce the same word many
times over — *tager*, *tog*, *taget* — at extra cost, and the entries would not necessarily
agree with each other. Collapsing the surfaces into lemmas first, and then generating one
entry per lemma that actually occurs in the book, is an optimisation rather than a
requirement: it just saves money, and makes the dictionary more consistent.

And an **entry** consists of one or more **senses**: the distinct meanings of the word.
*Gift*, for example, can mean both "poison" and "married".

## How I built it

I've been quiet on this blog, but in the meantime I've gotten a lot of practice at agentic
coding. I won't claim to be an expert, but I've found a workflow that works well for me.

I started by discussing the problem with Perplexity. For a project like this, a search-based
engine that also has access to strong models is the best starting point: it covers a lot of
blind spots. I didn't know how a dictionary is constructed, how to read an EPUB, or whether
this was even possible. My first question was simply: can this be done at all? The answer was
yes. Once I understood the terminology, I hammered out a rough plan with Perplexity.

Then I moved to a coding agent, through OpenRouter — I don't vibe-code often enough to
justify a Claude subscription, and this is cheap. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, for example, is very
cheap and very capable; it is roughly as capable as the absolute frontier from six months
ago, which is kind of insane.

I start with an overview of all the todos, no details, in an HTML plan that I can easily
inspect and edit, and that contains all the context an agent needs. Then I grill it: first to
make sure there are no blocking decisions and that the whole process is understood. The plan
is divided into phases, each one a logical unit that can be executed on its own, like a
ticket. For each phase I grill until I am satisfied that it says exactly what to do, and then
execute. From that point — especially for something no more complex than this — it is pretty
hands-off.

A few decisions mattered. Stanza, my first lemmatizer, was unreliable: it would often take a
surface form and produce a completely different lemma. The DDO full-form list turned out to
work much better — it has a neat entry for almost every word. I also tried adding cognates
(Dutch is close to Danish, so a cognate is much easier to remember) and morphology (word
composition), because both should help a learner. The models just aren't good at that: they
hallucinate, or produce nothing useful, especially when a word has several senses. So I
removed both.

## Picking a model

For the model choice, I asked Perplexity which models made sense. It suggested a few cheap
ones: a Qwen model, GPT-6 Luna, and Claude Haiku, all under $0.20 per million input tokens.
Output is cheap too, because every response is a small structured JSON object. And most of
the input is the same for every word — only the contexts change — so it is largely
cacheable.

Then I ran a bake-off: 200 entries, judged by Mistral Large 4 — chosen on the hunch that it
would be good at European languages. That is not evidence-based, but it gave a good enough
signal. Haiku won consistently, which makes sense since it was the newest of the three. That
is what I went with, and I'm quite happy with the results.

The whole thing took about four hours, and about $3 in tokens to produce a dictionary for one
more book. That is the marginal cost, not the total: the project as a whole cost closer to $6,
because I ran the pipeline twice and paid a judge model during the bake-off. Some entries are
imperfect — an odd sense here, a missing name there — but every word I have tapped so far has
had an entry.

## Reading with it

I am reading a Danish book, and with a dictionary that actually covers it I look up maybe two
or three words per page. That is few enough that it does not pull me out of the story, which
is the whole point. I genuinely enjoy reading it; it does not feel like a chore.

So my recommendation: once you are around B2/C1, start reading books for entertainment. Find
something at the right level, read it on an e-reader, and use a dictionary that has entries
for everything you need. If you already speak several languages, glosses in more than one of
them are genuinely helpful too — they make words easier to remember.

And that is the result I actually care about: I can read books in Danish now. The book I
started with is *Spektrum* — aimed at a younger audience, and I might have preferred something
more mature, but the writing is good and it is exactly the right level for me. More than
enough.
