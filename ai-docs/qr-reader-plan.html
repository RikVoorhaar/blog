Writing a QR code reader from scratch

Working slug qr-reader · in progress

This is a living document, not a contract. When the post disagrees with the plan, the post wins. Update the plan in the same commit as the change.

Goal

Tell the story of building one workable QR detector from scratch: the geometric problem, the pipeline, the dead ends and the version that finally worked. The detector is the main subject. The decoder is a short handoff, with an honest note about its largely vibe-coded implementation and the role of agentic development. This is not a survey, a QR specification tour, or a claim that this is the best possible approach.

Audience

Peer engineers and technically curious readers who are comfortable with Python, computer vision basics and some linear algebra. They want the practical reasoning and the failure modes, not a hand-held introduction to QR codes.

Algorithm orientation (refresher)

Short version: the corner finder went through three ideas. The first two are in the history, the third is the current production path. Choosing the triplet is a separate story with two genuinely different algorithms: pairwise edge colinearity, then centre geometry. The descriptions below are reconstructed from the current code and the old commits, not from memory.

1. Flood fill → boundary trace → angular NMS (first attempt, now unused)

This is the binary-image approach. The code still exists in detector/region.py and detector/corner.py, and the old scripts/full-pipeline.py used it per candidate cluster.

  1. Start from a candidate cluster. Use its centre and the first dark run of the 1:1:3:1:1 scan to seed the outer black ring of the finder.
  2. Flood fill the connected dark region with an 8-neighbour wave front (region_fill_wave_front).
  3. Take the boundary pixels of that region (region_boundary_8) and split them into connected components with scipy.ndimage.label.
  4. For each boundary component: compute its centroid, then radial distance and angle of every boundary pixel. Pick the 4 points with the largest radial distance, suppressing points within 10° of an already picked one (angular_nms_top_radial_indices).
  5. Those four points are the finder corners.

The appeal: it works directly on the binary image and needs no gradients or line fitting. The obvious weak points: it assumes a clean, contiguous dark ring; blur, noise, low contrast or a bad seed can fragment the region or merge it with something else; a connected boundary can have multiple components; and “the four farthest boundary points from the centroid” is only a good corner definition when the ring is clean and fairly square in the image. Under perspective the farthest points are not necessarily the corners. The hand-written NumPy wave front was later replaced by scipy.ndimage.binary_propagation. The speed discussion was dropped from the post; the benchmark remains in blog_figures/flood_fill/benchmark_fill.py.

The repository does not contain a written post-mortem for dropping this approach. The mechanics above are documented; the actual “what broke first” story is yours to rant about.

2. Gradient-guided Hough lines (second attempt, code removed)

This is the one that took the most work and was eventually deleted in commit f5451e8. The old reports are docs/report-hough-bottleneck.md and docs/hough-failure-analysis.md.

  1. On a grayscale ROI: Gaussian blur (σ = 1.0) → Sobel Gx, Gy → gradient magnitude → non-maximum suppression along the gradient direction. This is not Canny: there is no hysteresis, only thinned Sobel edges plus a gradient-normal angle per edge pixel.
  2. Each edge pixel votes in a (θ, ρ) Hough accumulator, using its gradient angle as the line normal. θ is folded modulo π. Final sweeps used about 0.5° × 0.5 px bins and weighted votes by edge strength.
  3. Peaks are picked iteratively: take the global maximum, accept it if it is at least 0.15 × the global maximum, suppress a neighbourhood (±5 bins), repeat up to 20 times.
  4. Refine each peak into a segment: collect edge pixels within 1.5 px of the line, optionally gate by gradient angle, fit a weighted total-least-squares line, project the support onto it, and keep the longest contiguous run with a small gap tolerance (2–3 px).
  5. Intersect the four outer side lines to get the finder corners. The old target was actually all 36 finder edges (12 per finder), with the outer quadrilateral as the practical product.

Why it was abandoned, in the repo's own words: it hit a hard recall ceiling around 0.928 because edge-pixel position jitter scatters votes across adjacent ρ bins, leaving the true bin empty. More parameter tuning could not fix that. The failure catalogue names four modes:

  • A — span too short: gap tolerance cannot bridge 3–7 px NMS gaps, so a true edge becomes a fragment.
  • B — phantom in blank: sparse coincidentally aligned pixels produce a line where no continuous edge exists.
  • C — span too long: TLS direction drift and the distance threshold capture a parallel module edge 3–5 px away.
  • D — edge missing: vote dilution and the relative threshold lose weak or fragmented true edges, especially the inward-facing finder edges.

On the v12 default fixture the reported tally was A=2, B=5, C=4, D=2, with 4 of 6 ground-truth edges in the ROIs matched within 5° and 5 px.

3. Ray-profile fitting (current production path)

Current entry point: fit_finder_ray in detector/ray_fit.py. Called per candidate cluster from detector/detector.py, with the grayscale ROI, the cluster centre and m_est = cluster_width / 7.

  1. Normalize the ROI. The intensities are mapped to [0, 1] using centre-weighted p10/p90, so the dark/bright split reflects the finder's own contrast rather than the background.
  2. Sample 36 half-rays. From the estimated centre, sample 120 bilinear points along each equally spaced direction, out to half the ROI diagonal.
  3. Fit one module pitch per ray. The finder cross-section is modelled as dark 3-module centre → white 1-module ring → dark 1-module ring → white quiet zone, with erfc softened transitions (σ = 1 px). For each ray, grid-search m in [m_est/2, 2·m_est] (50 values) against a masked MSE, then refine the winner with a bounded scalar search. Rays that fail are marked invalid.
  4. Turn the fits into boundary points. Each valid ray contributes one point at 3.5·m modules from the centre, i.e. on the finder's outer boundary. This gives 36 points in cyclic order, with NaNs for failed rays.
  5. Cluster the points into four sides. Phase 0 starts with every adjacent point pair as a cluster. Nearby pairs are compared by the TLS degeneracy ratio σ₂/σ₁ on their union: points on the same straight edge are near-colinear (low ratio); points on different edges are not. Phase 1 uses single-linkage agglomerative clustering on that distance matrix. Phase 2 keeps the four largest clusters and fits a TLS line to each.
  6. Reject non-finders. The top four clusters must contain at least 50% of all valid boundary points; otherwise the candidate is not a finder and valid=False.
  7. Intersect the four lines. The corners are the pairwise intersections of the left, right, top and bottom lines. The score is the fraction of rays that fitted successfully times 1 − mean(σ₂/σ₁).

There is also an optional projective joint refinement (refine_finder_edges_joint) that models all four sides and the known transition positions together with a Levenberg–Marquardt fit. It exists in the code but is not enabled by the production call: fit_finder_ray defaults to refine_joint=False.

Why this is a different bet: it never asks edge pixels to vote into a global (θ, ρ) grid. It uses the known radial structure of the finder, lets each ray estimate its own local module pitch, and then only has to group 36 points into four lines. That handles local scale changes and perspective much better than a single global line-voting step.

4. Choosing the triplet: edge colinearity → centre geometry

Current entry point: find_valid_triplets in detector/finder_pattern.py, called from detector/detector.py after ray fitting and deduplication. It replaced the pairwise-association route in d7ec775 and was simplified to corner-derived geometry in 2e53f22.

Draft intro. Suppose we have found three finder patterns and their corners. Which one is the top-left, which is the top-right, and which is the bottom-left? We do not assume the QR code is upright — it could be rotated by any angle, including 180°. And if we found more than three candidates, which three belong to the same QR code? We need an algorithm for this, and as with everything else in this project, it is trickier than it appears at first glance.

Why a triplet at all. Three plausible finder patterns are not enough. There can be false positives, and a single finder is almost square with fourfold rotational symmetry, so it cannot tell you which edge is “up” or “right.” The triplet supplies the global context: pick the corner of the L, then use the two inter-centre vectors to assign top-left, top-right and bottom-left.

Figure decision: the intro figure shows the ambiguity — four or more candidate finder patterns with corners, and no correct triplet marked.

Attempt 1 — pairwise outer-edge colinearity (f3f1a1d). For every pair of finder patterns, compare their four outer edges. If two pairs of edges are nearly parallel and nearly collinear, the finder patterns are “associated.” Build an association graph, then look for an L-shape: finder B is connected to A and C, but A and C are not connected, and the associations use different edges of B. Then B is the top-left finder.

  • check_association used angle_tol = 0.1 rad and offset_tol = 0.15, and required exactly two passing colinear segment pairs.
  • max_offset normalized the endpoint-to-line distance by the distance between the two segment midpoints. That makes the score depend on the distance between finder patterns, not just on their local geometry.
  • At high QR versions the finders stay roughly the same pixel size but get farther apart, so wrong cross-pairs get a smaller normalized offset and start passing too.

Concrete failure from docs/find-all-associations-debug.md, version 12, seed 0: 

Pair FP 0 <-> FP 1: REJECT: expected exactly 2 colinear pairs, got 4
colinear_pairs: [(0, 0), (0, 2), (2, 0), (2, 2)]

The two true pairs had offsets around 0.020 and 0.021. The two false cross-pairs had offsets around 0.110 and 0.139, both below the 0.15 threshold. At version 4 the same false pairs had offsets around 0.25–0.30 and were rejected. Result: production found fewer than the two needed associations from version 8 onward, and zero associations for versions 9–12.

Repair — local normalization and best-two pairing (e335de8). The fix was to normalize the offset by the mean length of the two segments, raise offset_tol to 0.30, try both opposite-side axes, try both one-to-one pairings, and select the best valid pair of pairs. Segment indices are not consistent across finders, so the correct pairing can be (0, 1), (2, 3) rather than (0, 0), (2, 2). This recovered two associations for every version from 1 through 12 in the synthetic sweep, and also worked across high-version seeds. It was still relying on exact outer-corner geometry.

Why it was abandoned — too many missed triplets. The local-normalization fix solved the synthetic high-version case, but the method was still too brittle on real images. It missed too many true triplets, and loosening the thresholds only let more false associations through. After the corner fitter changed, the detector tests were 16 failed, 2 passed, all with No finder-pattern triplet found; raising offset_tol to 1.20 did not recover it. The problem was the invariant: it assumed the outer edges were accurate enough to line up across the whole code. They were not. No amount of threshold tweaking made that reliable.

Do not use the per-finder homography as the reason this approach died. That was a separate corner-fitting experiment: it improved local corner accuracy under perspective, but it did not fix the triplet problem, and the association was already failing before it. Keep it out of the triplet story.

Attempt 2 — centre geometry (d7ec775, simplified 2e53f22). The current algorithm does not compare edges at all. It asks whether three finder centres form the QR code’s right triangle at compatible scales.

  • Pair connectivity: module pitches must satisfy |m_i - m_j| / max(m_i, m_j) < 0.3; centre distance must be between 2 * m_avg and 200 * m_avg; and the inter-centre vector must be aligned with one corner-edge direction (max(|dot|) > 0.9) and nearly perpendicular to the other (min(|dot|) < 0.25 * max(|dot|)).
  • Triplet discovery: a finder with at least two neighbours forms a candidate when its two neighbours are not directly connected, the angle at that finder is within 15° of a right angle, and all three module sizes agree within 30%.
  • Orientation: the corner finder is the top-left; the cross product of the two inter-centre vectors assigns top-right and bottom-left. The cross product is computed in (x, y) = (col, row) coordinates. Commit 7f91833 fixed a sign-convention bug that had swapped top-right and bottom-left.
  • Geometry source: the original version used the per-finder FinderFit fields center, e1, e2, m. After the ray fit replaced fit_finder_full, the current version derives them from the four outer corners: module pitch is the mean outer side length divided by 7, and the two axes are adjacent corner edges.

Tradeoffs and current limits. The 15° right-angle gate assumes moderate perspective; a strongly skewed code can violate it. The code does not require the two legs of the L to be equal length, only compatible module sizes. And detector.py takes raw_triplets[0] — the first valid triplet, not the best-scoring one. With one QR code in frame that has been enough, but it is not the most robust possible choice.

The captured benchmark makes this stage visible: 64 of 100 photos produce at least three finder patterns, but only 49 produce a valid triplet. Across the 51 detection failures, 35 are recorded as No finder-pattern triplet found. So this is still the main gate in the pipeline.

5. Estimating the homography (current production path)

Current entry point: _run_detection in detector/detector.py, with the geometry in detector/homography.py and the sampler in detector/sample.py. The homography maps QR-grid coordinates (x, y) in module units to image pixel coordinates (x, y).

Provenance. This is not the version-estimation path that came out of the triplet work. The geometric estimate used the three finder module pitches and the three inter-finder distances to compute N_est, then searched only N_legal ± 4. That code is visible at 7ecc449 and earlier. Commit 809aa8d removed it and replaced it with the full 21..177 search plus the timing score. The timing score first appears in 7ecc449, whose message cites quirc's grid-fitness approach. An earlier diagnostic plan (docs/plan-015-baseline/diagnostic_plan.md:117) explicitly said timing-pattern scoring was out of scope because “existing version estimation already works.” So the current design is an agent-introduced change, not something specified in the triplet plan.

The tracked benchmark before 809aa8d shows why the old estimate may have been abandoned: with the old fit_finder_full module pitch, exact version accuracy among detections was 2/20 for v1, 15/15 for v3, 8/10 for v5, 6/6 for v8, 4/9 for v12 and 3/18 for v20. The v1 cases were usually off by one (v2), and v20 was often off by more than one. That benchmark predates the ray fit, so it does not tell us how the current corner-derived module pitch would perform.

I checked the old shortcut against the current pipeline. On the captured photos, the three-candidate window around N_est agrees with the full search on 46 of 47 detections. The one disagreement is qr_code_ - 15.jpeg, which does not decode anyway. On the synthetic set, full and narrow differ in 1 of 186 detections (a v20 case where N_est is off by six modules). This does not prove the shortcut is universally safe, but it does show the full forty-version search is not necessary for most of the current data.

Draft intro. At this point we have three finder patterns and their four outer corners. That is twelve point correspondences: we know where each corner sits in the image, and we know where it sits in the QR grid. A homography is exactly the object that turns one into the other. The only catch is that we do not yet know the QR version, so we do not know how far apart the finder patterns are in grid coordinates. We solve both problems at once: try every legal version, fit a homography for each, and keep the one whose finder geometry and timing pattern agree best.

Why a homography. The QR code is a planar grid viewed by a camera, so the mapping from grid to image is a projective transformation. A homography has eight degrees of freedom (the 3×3 matrix is defined up to scale), so four point correspondences are enough in principle. We have twelve, which gives redundancy and lets us average out corner noise.

The twelve correspondences. For a candidate module count N = 17 + 4V, the three finder patterns occupy known blocks of the grid: 

TL finder: (0, 0), (7, 0), (7, 7), (0, 7)
TR finder: (N-7, 0), (N, 0), (N, 7), (N-7, 7)
BL finder: (0, N-7), (0, N), (7, N), (7, N-7)

The corner order from the ray fit is arbitrary, so _canonicalize_corners first sorts each finder's four corners into TL, TR, BR, BL using the global axes defined by the triplet centres (top-left → top-right, top-left → bottom-left). Without that step the correspondences would be scrambled.

  1. Normalize the points. DLT is numerically unhappy when the two coordinate systems have very different scales (module units versus thousands of pixels). Both point sets are translated so their centroid is at the origin and scaled so their mean distance from the origin is √2 (Hartley normalization).
  2. Solve the linear system. Each correspondence gives two rows of a 2n × 9 matrix A. The homography is the null vector of A, found as the last row of Vᵀ in an SVD. Undo the two normalization transforms and scale so H[2, 2] = 1.
  3. Refine with Levenberg–Marquardt. The DLT solution minimizes an algebraic error, not the reprojection error we actually care about. refine_homography_lm optimizes the eight free parameters directly against the mean squared distance between the projected grid corners and the measured image corners, using scipy.optimize.least_squares with method="lm", an analytical Jacobian and a linear loss.
  4. Score the timing pattern. For each candidate N, project grid row 6 and grid column 6 (the timing patterns, modules 8 through N-9) into the image, sample the grayscale, threshold at the mean, and count how often the sampled bits alternate. A correct scale gives a clean alternation; a wrong scale smears the pattern. The score is the fraction of possible alternations, in [0, 1].
  5. Pick the version. The loop tries all forty legal sizes N = 21, 25, …, 177. For each it computes the mean reprojection error err and the timing score timing, then minimizes err - timing. The units do not match: err is in pixels and timing is in [0, 1]. In practice the reprojection error does most of the work. The 12-point fit includes the three 7-module finder widths, so a wrong N forces the same image corners to be both 7 modules wide and the wrong distance apart, which produces a large error. The timing score is a secondary check and is noisy enough to prefer a neighbouring version on its own. This is a heuristic objective, not a calibrated likelihood.

Why the timing pattern is the version check. The timing pattern is a ruler that runs between the finder patterns and alternates every module. It is present in every QR version and does not require reading the version information first. The finder corners alone tell us the local scale near the three corners; the timing pattern tells us whether the global scale implied by a particular N is consistent across the code. For a wrong N, the projected row and column are supposed to land on the wrong modules and collapse the alternation.

In practice the timing score is noisy. On a v1 photo it preferred v2, and on a v10 photo it preferred v9, while the 12-point reprojection error picked the correct version in both cases. The finder-size consistency is what actually pins the version down; the timing pattern is a sanity check, not the primary estimator. That is worth saying honestly in the post, because the timing-pattern idea sounds cleverer than it turned out to be.

Why not RANSAC? ransac_homography exists in the module, but the production path does not use it. The twelve corners have already been filtered by the finder fitter and the triplet gate; there are no gross outliers left to reject. Plain DLT plus LM is enough, and it is simpler to reason about. RANSAC was useful while the corner fitter was noisier, but it is not part of the current pipeline.

Handoff to sampling. Once we have H and N, sample_qr_bits projects every module centre through H, samples a 3×3 neighbourhood per module, and takes a weighted majority vote (centre weight 2, surrounding weight 1). The black/white threshold is calibrated from the known finder-pattern cells: the midpoint between the median dark and median light values. So the homography does not have to be perfect everywhere; the sampler only needs the projected module centre to land inside the right module.

Tradeoffs and current limits. The twelve points all sit in three corners of the code. The bottom-right corner is never observed directly; it is extrapolated by the homography. Under strong perspective or at high versions, the extrapolated grid can drift, and sampling errors concentrate in the bottom-right. This is exactly the job the alignment patterns would do, and we skipped them. The timing score only checks two lines (row 6 and column 6), not the bottom-right corner, so a slightly wrong N can still score well on a small code. The version information in the QR symbol (versions 7 and up) is read by the decoder as a cross-check, not by the detector to choose N; if N is wrong, the sampled matrix is already the wrong size.

One implementation quirk worth remembering: the timing score samples integer grid coordinates (module boundaries) rather than module centres. It still produces a usable relative score because the pattern alternates, but it is not a calibrated measurement. If this stage is ever revisited, sampling at (x + 0.5, 6.5) would be the natural fix.

Benchmark evidence. On the 100 captured photos, 49 images reach a valid triplet and all 49 also produce a homography and a version. The funnel then drops to 40 decodable and 40 correct payloads. So homography estimation is not the stage that loses most images in the current benchmark; the bottleneck is earlier (triplet finding) and the remaining losses are sampling/decoder failures. Two of the nine decode failures are version mismatches, which can mean a wrong N or a corrupted version-info region; that is a useful reminder that the version search is a heuristic, not a guarantee.

Do not blame the homography for the triplet stage's failures. The pipeline takes raw_triplets[0], the first valid triplet, not the best-scoring one. If that triplet is wrong, the homography is fit to the wrong three finders and can still report success with a plausible version. That is a triplet-selection problem, not a DLT problem.

Figure decision: the obvious figure is the twelve-point correspondence — three finder patterns with their corners marked, the grid coordinates they map to, and the projected QR grid overlaid on the image. A second candidate is the version search: reprojection error and timing score versus candidate version, with the chosen version marked. That plot is interesting because it shows the error picking the right version while the timing score prefers a neighbour. A third, more honest figure is the bottom-right drift on a strong-perspective or high-version photo. Decide which of these earn space; the correspondence figure is the one that explains the method.

Facts & numbers

  • Version V is 17 + 4V modules per side; version 1 is 21×21, version 40 is 177×177. Each finder pattern is 7 modules across.
  • Finder ratio test: 1:1:3:1:1, each run within 30% of ideal; clustering uses 22% segment-width agreement and 1.2× height distance.
  • Old pairwise association at v12/seed 0: true segment offsets ≈ 0.020 and 0.021; false cross-pairs ≈ 0.110 and 0.139; threshold was 0.15. At v4 the false cross-pairs were ≈ 0.25–0.30. Source: docs/find-all-associations-debug.md.
  • Current triplet gates: module-size agreement within 30%, centre distance between 2× and 200× mean module pitch, inter-centre axis alignment max(|dot|) > 0.9 with the perpendicular component below 25% of that, and the angle at the corner finder within 15° of 90°.
  • Homography input: 12 outer corners, four from each of the three finder patterns. For a candidate module count N, those corners map to the grid blocks (0..7, 0..7), (N-7..N, 0..7) and (0..7, N-7..N). The homography has eight free parameters; DLT is solved by SVD after Hartley normalization, then refined by LM on reprojection error.
  • Version search: all forty legal sizes N = 17 + 4V for V = 1..40 are tried. The timing-pattern score samples row 6 and column 6 from module 8 to N-9, thresholds at the mean and counts bit flips; the final score is mean_reprojection_error - timing_score. The units do not match: the error is in pixels and the timing term is in [0, 1]. In practice the reprojection error dominates because a wrong N creates a large scale mismatch at the 7-module finders. The timing score is a noisy secondary check; on a v1 photo it preferred v2, and on a v10 photo it preferred v9, while the reprojection error picked the correct version in both cases.
  • Version-score examples from the current code: on qr_code_ - 99.jpeg (v1), the correct N=21 has err=1.60 px and timing=0.50; N=25 has err=36.3 px and timing=0.625. On qr_code_ - 16.jpeg (v10), N=57 has err=0.82 px and timing=0.46; N=53 has err=7.53 px and timing=0.92. The reprojection error picks the right version; the timing score does not. Source: temporary score-curve diagnostic, not a committed script.
  • Captured benchmark funnel: 65 → 64 → 49 → 49 → 40 → 40 out of 100 images (candidates, finders, triplets, homography/version, decodable, correct payload). Source: benchmark_captured.json. The homography stage itself loses none of the 49 triplets; the remaining nine failures are sampling/decoder failures, including two version-mismatch errors.
  • Synthetic benchmark: benchmark_current.json has 180 cases with 78 detection successes and 52 decodes; benchmark_ray.json has 180 cases with 62 detections and 60 decodes. Local ignored outputs; verify before quoting.
  • The current detector does not narrow the version to a range and then try candidates during decoding. It fits a homography for every legal N and picks the lowest combined reprojection/timing score. The decoder only cross-checks the version information for versions 7 and up, after sampling. The earlier “try a range during decoding” idea is not what the code does now.
